Interactive companion to a paper in Social Sciences & Humanities Open, vol. 14 (2026)

Elite Participation as a Measure of Global Development

If world poverty and income gaps between countries have really shrunk, more countries should be able to compete at the global frontier of activities that need surplus resources, health, infrastructure and international networks. This site lets you explore who has made it into fifteen global elite sets since 1970, from chess, tennis and Olympic swimming to supercomputers, fine dining, Nobel prizes and the most-cited research papers, and how the number of countries with a national football team has grown.

Swimming, Nobel prizes and research papers were added after publication and are not in the published paper. They are marked Not in paper throughout the site.

For a short, non-technical introduction, read the blog post Measuring Global Convergence Without Measuring Income.

Science: Nobel laureates and the most-cited research Not in paper

Neither series in this section is in the published paper. Both were added afterwards to see whether science follows the pattern of the paper's ten elite sets.

Nobel laureates: where they work and where they were born

Nobel prizes in physics, chemistry, medicine and economics show a different pattern from sport and technology. Where laureates do their prize-winning careers has stayed highly concentrated, with the United States far ahead, while their countries of birth are much more varied. The gap is a measure of how strongly a few research systems attract talent from elsewhere.

Two cautions: prizes usually reward work done decades earlier, so they describe the research elite of a generation ago, and each 5-year window has only 33 to 52 laureates.

The 500 most-cited research papers of the year

For every fifth year from 1970 to 2020, the 500 most-cited works published that year in OpenAlex, counted by the authors' countries of affiliation. Each paper counts as one, split between the countries of its authors. Unlike Nobel prizes, the research frontier has spread out: the US share has fallen from about two thirds to under a third, China has risen from almost nothing to a quarter, and more than 80 countries had a share of at least one top paper in 2020.

Two cautions: country data are missing for many older papers (shown as hollow markers), and 2020 is dominated by COVID-19 research. Without the 227 papers with COVID in the title, 2020 still shows the same picture: concentration 0.14, diversity 0.50, 76 countries, US 33 percent and China 10 percent.

Almost every country now has a national football team, women's included

A national team is a far lower bar than a place in a top-25 ranking. For men, the measure is FIFA membership. For women, it is the share of countries whose team had played an official international match (up to 2000), and from 2005 the share with a team in the FIFA Women's World Ranking. Shares can exceed 100 percent because some FIFA members are not sovereign states, and the United Kingdom has four teams.

Method and data

Indices

With pi the share of country i in the elite set and N the number of sovereign countries in the world that year (166 in 1970, 198 in 2025):

H = Σ pi²,   H* = (H − 1/N) / (1 − 1/N)
S = −Σ pi ln pi,   S* = S / ln N

Lower H* means less concentration; higher S* means more diversity. The unadjusted indices give the same picture (Appendix Table A2 of the paper).

Coding

  • Country is nationality for players, venue for restaurants and institutional location for supercomputers.
  • Tennis players are coded by nationality at the time, so the Soviet Union, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia appear before they dissolved.
  • West Germany is shown as Germany, as in the source data; East Germany appears separately.
  • In golf, England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland are coded as the United Kingdom.
  • Series start when a ranking exists: men's golf 1986, women's golf 2006, restaurants 2004, supercomputers 1993 (top 50) and 1995 (top 500).

Limitations

  • The poorest countries rarely appear in any elite set, and Africa is almost absent. The indicators describe how elite capacity spreads beyond traditional centres more than progress at the bottom.
  • Each domain has its own conditions, such as snow for alpine skiing and FIFA development funding for football.
  • Nationality does not show where skills were developed, and the restaurant list rests on the judgement of a voting panel.

Additions not in the published paper

Swimming, Nobel prizes and research papers were added to this site after publication. They use the same indices but are not part of the paper's analysis.

  • Swimming counts the eight finalists in each of the 11 individual pool events held at every Olympics since 1972, 88 per sex. The boycotted 1980 and 1984 Games are replaced by the 1978 and 1982 World Championships. Swimmers competing as neutral athletes are coded by their own country.
  • Nobel prizes cover physics, chemistry, medicine and economics, grouped in 5-year windows (1971–75 to 2021–25) because each year has only a few laureates. "Where laureates work" uses the affiliation at the award, split equally when a laureate had affiliations in several countries; four laureates without an affiliation are coded by residence, and Myrdal and Hayek (1974) are left out. "Country of birth" uses present-day borders.
  • Research papers are the 500 most-cited works published in each fifth year from 1970 to 2020 in OpenAlex (citations counted in November 2026), keeping the 461 to 488 journal articles among them. Each paper counts as one, split equally between authors with a known country, and each author's share split across their countries. Hong Kong and Macao are counted with China and other territories with their sovereign state; before 1991, republics of the Soviet Union, Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia are merged into those states.
  • OpenAlex lacks affiliation countries for many older papers: 62 to 79 percent of papers have country data in 1970–1990 (hollow markers) and 84 to 97 percent from 1995. Older papers have also had longer to collect citations.

Download

The data behind this site, in long format. For Nobel prizes, "year" is the end of the 5-year window, "rank" the prize year, and "weight" the laureate's share when affiliations span several countries. For research papers, each row is a country, "weight" its fractional number of papers and "rank" its order within the year.
